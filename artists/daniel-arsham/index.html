Artist Market Profiles

Daniel Arsham

Daniel Arsham is an American artist born in Cleveland in 1980 and raised in Miami, according to Highsnobiety and Ocula. He casts everyday objects, from Brillo boxes to Porsches and Pokémon, in sand, selenite, quartz and volcanic ash so that they look like finds from a future dig, a series he calls Future Relics.

About the artist

The table lists the five most recent sales we could document, newest first. One is a large unique sculpture at Phillips in New York, one a crystal Pokémon sculpture in Seoul, and three are pieces from editions of 500. Every figure here comes from MutualArt's sale records, read on 26 November 2026, unless we say we checked the auction house's own page. We checked the Phillips result on Phillips' own lot page.

Phillips' $154,800 for Rose Quartz Eroded Giuliano di Medici is the price with fees and more than two and a half times the top estimate of $60,000. The LLB Veiled Porsche price is a hammer price, as MutualArt states it excludes the premium. MutualArt also lists the same lot a second time at €3,750, which is €3,000 plus 25%, so treat that as the same sale, not a second one. The Seoul prices have no stated basis.

The edition sizes matter. Pieces from editions of 500 sold for between about KRW 1.2 million and €3,000 in November. The Phillips sculpture sold for more than forty times the dearest of them.

Life and career

Hurricane Andrew destroyed his family home when he was 12, according to Sotheby's. He studied at the Cooper Union and won a Gelman Trust Fellowship in 2003, according to Ocula. He co-founded the design studio Snarkitecture with Alex Mustonen; Ocula dates it to 2007.

A trip to Easter Island in 2011 started the fictional archaeology of his later work, according to Ocula. He is colour blind, and lenses he tried in 2015 let him see pinks and purples, which then came into the work, according to Sotheby's. In 2020 the Cleveland Cavaliers made him creative director, according to Ocula.

Why the work is valued

Arsham is represented by Perrotin, according to Ocula. He designed sets for Merce Cunningham and has worked with Dior, Adidas, Porsche, Hedi Slimane and Pharrell Williams, according to Sotheby's. In 2020 he showed Moonraker at the Musée Guimet in Paris, according to Ocula and Sotheby's.

Much of his public profile comes from these collaborations, which also explains the size of his editions. The auction record for his unique sculpture is a better guide to how collectors rate the work itself.

Reading the prices

Count the edition. A Porsche or Pikachu from an edition of 500 is a different product from a unique quartz figure, even when the shape looks familiar.

Duplicate records happen. Online sales often appear twice in databases, once at the hammer price and once with the premium. Check the lot number and the date before counting a sale twice.

These figures are a record of past public sales. They are not a valuation and they are not advice. For how to read them, start with what an auction result actually tells collectors.
