David Hockney

David Hockney was born in Bradford on 9 July 1937 and died at home on 11 June 2026, aged 88, according to The Art Newspaper. He painted swimming pools in California, double portraits of friends and, late in life, Yorkshire and Normandy landscapes, many of them drawn on an iPad.

About the artist

The table lists the five most recent sales we could document, newest first, all at Phillips in New York. Four sold within three days in November 2026. David, Celia, Stephen and Ian, London 1982, an oil painting in eight parts, sold in the evening sale on 18 November for US$12,200,000 with premium, inside its estimate of US$10 million to 15 million.

Two days later the morning session offered three very different Hockneys. Lemons & Oranges, a small oil of about 1986, made US$645,000. Summer Road Near Kilham (2008), an inkjet-printed computer drawing numbered 6 from an edition of 25, made US$180,600, just above its low estimate. An untitled print of 1987, made by Hockney himself on an office colour copier and unique, made US$28,380.

In October, a complete artist's proof set of The Blue Guitar etchings (1976 to 1977) sold for US$51,600, and in Phillips' London online sale Esso Tripoli, a 1968 pencil and crayon drawing, sold for £25,800. Extending (1990), a home-made print numbered 21 from an edition of 50, shows no price on its lot page.

Life and career

He studied at Bradford School of Art from 1953 to 1957 and at the Royal College of Art from 1959 to 1962, choosing it over the Slade. While a student he showed in Young Contemporaries in 1961, and made the etching suite A Rake's Progress between 1961 and 1963.

He first visited California in 1963 and spent much of the next forty years there, according to Tate. The pool paintings date from those years, as do the large double portraits such as Mr and Mrs Clark and Percy (1970 to 1971).

From 2004 he lived and worked in Bridlington, on the Yorkshire coast, painting the Wolds in works such as Bigger Trees Near Warter (2007). During the Covid-19 pandemic he moved to Normandy, where he drew on the iPad. He won the John Moores Painting Prize in 1967.

Why the work is valued

Tate holds A Bigger Splash (1967), The Third Love Painting (1960) and George Lawson and Wayne Sleep (1972 to 1975). He was made a Royal Academician in 1991, a Companion of Honour in 1997 and a member of the Order of Merit in 2012. In 2021 the Royal Academy showed 116 of his Normandy iPad drawings as The Arrival of Spring.

He worked in almost every medium, including prints he made himself on an office colour copier. So the name alone does not tell you whether a lot is a painting, a drawing, an edition or a unique home-made print.

Key works, and what reaches auction

A Bigger Splash (1967)

Acrylic on cotton duck, about 2.5 metres square. Tate

Tate explains that he stapled the canvas to the wall, rolled on the flat areas and spent about two weeks on the splash itself, working from someone else's photograph. The rest of the picture is still and empty, which is what makes the splash read.

A Rake's Progress (1961 to 1963)

Suite of 16 etchings

Made while he was still a student at the Royal College of Art, according to The Art Newspaper. Prints have been a large part of his market ever since, which is why editions appear in almost every Hockney sale.

Summer Road Near Kilham (2008)

Inkjet-printed computer drawing, edition of 25

A Yorkshire Wolds subject made on a computer and printed by inkjet. It sold at Phillips New York on 20 November 2026 and shows how his late digital work reaches the market in editions.

Reading the prices

All five results in the table are Phillips' own figures and include the buyer's premium. They run from US$28,380 to US$12.2 million because they are five different kinds of object.

Several of these lots sold within months of his death. We make no claim about whether his death affected prices. Five results do not show that, one way or the other.

These figures are a record of past public sales. They are not a valuation and they are not advice. For how to read them, start with what an auction result actually tells collectors.
