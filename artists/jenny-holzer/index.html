Artist Market Profiles

Jenny Holzer

Jenny Holzer is an American artist born in Gallipolis, Ohio, in 1950, who has worked with words since the late 1970s, according to the Guggenheim in Venice. Her texts have appeared on street posters, LED signs, stone benches and light projections, and smaller editions such as stamp sets and engraved objects come up at auction often.

About the artist

The table lists the five most recent sales we could document, newest first. Four are small editions: two rubber stamp sets, an engraved glass ball and a screenprint on wood. The fifth, a bronze plaque from her Survival series reading Protect me from what I want, made $70,400 at Christie's against an estimate of $10,000 to 15,000. It came from the collection of Agnes Gund, a well-known collector, which often pushes prices up. Every figure here comes from MutualArt's sale records, read on 26 November 2026, unless we say we checked the auction house's own page.

Three prices are stated hammer prices (Bruun Rasmussen, Nosbüsch & Stucke and LLB). The Christie's and Seoul Auction figures have no stated basis. The Truisms Stamp Set at Nosbüsch & Stucke sold for €450 against a single estimate of €300.

Larger works do appear. On 23 October 2026 Christie's in Paris sold a 1989 granite bench from the Living series, number two of three, for €102,400 against an estimate of €60,000 to 80,000, according to MutualArt.

Life and career

She graduated from Ohio University in 1972, then studied at the Rhode Island School of Design and on the Whitney Museum's study programme in New York, according to the Guggenheim in Venice.

After the Truisms came the Inflammatory Essays (1979 to 1982), the Living series (1981 to 1982) and the Survival series (1983 to 1985), according to Britannica. Her first electronic sign went up in Times Square in 1982, according to Wikipedia, and in 2005 she started the Redaction paintings, based on declassified government documents, according to Britannica.

Why the work is valued

Holzer's installation for the US Pavilion at the 1990 Venice Biennale won the Golden Lion, according to Britannica and the Guggenheim in Venice. MoMA owns a version of her Truisms.

She began the Truisms in 1977 as anonymous posters pasted around Manhattan, according to MoMA and Wikipedia, and from 1996 moved to large outdoor light projections, according to Britannica.

Reading the prices

Provenance can add a lot. The Agnes Gund plaque sold for more than four times its top estimate. A famous previous owner is one reason buyers pay above estimate.

Small editions are an easy way in. Stamp sets and printed cards sell for a few hundred euros, but they are not a guide to what her stone and LED works fetch.
