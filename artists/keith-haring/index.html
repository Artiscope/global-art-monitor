Artist Market Profiles

Keith Haring

Keith Haring was born in Reading, Pennsylvania, on 4 May 1958 and died in New York on 16 February 1990, aged 31. He made his name in 1981 with chalk drawings on the empty black advertising panels of the New York subway, and went on to paint murals, canvases and prints in the same bold outline style.

Global Art Monitor has documented 3 auction lots by Keith Haring, offered between 17 September 2026 and 18 November 2026 at Phillips and Sotheby's. The most recent on our record is Untitled (1988), Phillips, 18 November 2026, sold for US$1,161,000 with buyer's premium. Source: Phillips lot page.

About the artist

We could document three sales with prices confirmed on the auction house's own lot page, all at Phillips in 2026. They are listed newest first. The most recent is a 1.5 metre canvas painted on 21 November 1988, which sold in Phillips' New York evening sale on 18 November 2026 for US$1,161,000 with premium, inside its estimate of US$900,000 to 1.2 million.

Two nights earlier a Haring was among the five unsold lots in Sotheby's Now and Contemporary Evening Auction in New York, according to The Art Newspaper. The report does not name the work, so it is not in the table.

Prints sell often. Phillips' results list shows three lots from its New York editions sale of 22 to 24 October 2026, including two sheets from the Apocalypse suite of 1988, made with William Burroughs, but the lot pages we read still showed the pre-sale view, so those figures are left out. The King (1989), a lithograph numbered 8 from an edition of 50, sold in London in September for £9,675 and is in the table.

Life and career

He grew up in Kutztown, Pennsylvania, where his father was an amateur cartoonist. He spent two semesters at the Ivy School of Professional Art in Pittsburgh and had a first solo show at the Pittsburgh Center for the Arts in 1978, according to PBS. The same year he moved to New York to study at the School of Visual Arts, and became part of the scene at Club 57 alongside Kenny Scharf and Basquiat.

The subway drawings began in 1981. PBS counts them in the hundreds over five years. In 1982 he had a solo show at Tony Shafrazi Gallery in SoHo and painted a mural at Houston Street and the Bowery. Public commissions followed across the world, from a section of the Berlin Wall in 1986 to Pisa in 1989.

Why the work is valued

Haring was shown in the 1983 Whitney Biennial. His public murals include Crack is Wack (1986) in New York and Tuttomondo (1989) in Pisa, painted on the wall of a convent beside the church of Sant'Antonio. He set up the Keith Haring Foundation in 1989, a year after his AIDS diagnosis.

He opened the Pop Shop in New York in 1986 to sell inexpensive merchandise carrying his imagery. That decision, which PBS sums up in his phrase art is for everyone, is one reason his name appears on a wide range of objects at very different prices.

Key works, and what reaches auction

Subway drawings (1981 to 1986)

White chalk on black paper panels in the New York subway

Unauthorised drawings on unused advertising panels, according to Britannica. The Jordan Schnitzer Museum of Art shows Untitled (Still Alive in '85), drawn on three joined sheets still on their transit authority mount. Surviving subway drawings are rare and are a market of their own.

Crack is Wack (1986)

Mural, New York

Listed by PBS among the works of 1986, the year he also opened the Pop Shop and painted on the Berlin Wall. Its title states the message plainly, as his public murals usually did.

Tuttomondo (1989)

Mural, Pisa

One of his last large public works, painted on the wall of the Servi di Maria convent between the railway and bus stations. The city's tourism office describes its subject as universal peace.

Reading the prices

Three confirmed results are a small sample, and they cover three different kinds of object: a late canvas, a 1981 drawing on Plexiglas and an editioned lithograph. They cannot be lined up as a series.

For prints, check the edition and the proof marks. A sheet marked HC or PP is a proof outside the numbered edition, and houses treat it separately.

These figures are a record of past public sales. They are not a valuation and they are not advice. For how to read them, start with what an auction result actually tells collectors.
