Artist Market Profiles

William Kentridge

William Kentridge is a South African artist born in Johannesburg in 1955, best known for prints, drawings and animated films made by drawing in charcoal, filming, rubbing out and filming again, according to Wikipedia. His market is split between Johannesburg and Cape Town salerooms and the big houses in New York and London.

About the artist

The table lists the five most recent sales we could document, newest first. Three come from one evening at Strauss & Co in Cape Town on 17 November 2026, one from Sotheby's in New York and one from Stephan Welz in Johannesburg. Every figure here comes from MutualArt's sale records, read on 26 November 2026, unless we say we checked the auction house's own page.

Only the Stephan Welz figure is a stated hammer price; the lithograph DOVE sold for ZAR 36,000 against an estimate of ZAR 70,000 to 90,000. The Strauss & Co and Sotheby's prices have no stated basis on MutualArt. The bronze Untitled VIII (Nose on Horseback) made $53,760 against $30,000 to 50,000.

We left out one more recent lot, a group of four collage works from 2016 sold on 25 November 2026 for €53,760, because we could not confirm the auction house before publishing. We will add it once we can.

Life and career

His parents were lawyers who represented people marginalised by apartheid, according to Wikipedia. He studied politics and African studies at the University of the Witwatersrand, then art at the Johannesburg Art Foundation, and theatre at the Lecoq school in Paris (same source).

In 1989 he began the films later known as Drawings for Projection with Johannesburg, 2nd Greatest City After Paris; Felix in Exile followed in 1994 (same source). He has since worked in tapestry, sculpture, opera and theatre.

Why the work is valued

Kentridge won the Kyoto Prize in 2010, had a solo show at the Royal Academy in London in 2022, and won an Olivier Award in 2023 for the opera Sibyl, according to Wikipedia.

In 2016 he made Triumphs and Laments, a 550-metre frieze of more than 80 figures cleaned into the grime of the Tiber embankment in Rome with water jets and stencils, according to The Art Newspaper. It cannot be sold and was meant to fade.

Reading the prices

Home markets matter. Three of these five sales were in South Africa, where Strauss & Co's evening sales give a good read of demand. Prices there are in rand, so check the exchange rate before comparing with New York.

Proofs are not editions. Printer's and artist's proofs sit outside the numbered edition. They are often valued like the edition prints, but buyers should know which they are getting.

These figures are a record of past public sales. They are not a valuation and they are not advice. For how to read them, start with what an auction result actually tells collectors.
