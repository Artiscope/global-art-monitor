The Buyer's Premium Explained, With the 2026 Rates

By the Global Art Monitor research desk. Published 23 November 2026. 8 minute read.

Most auction results you read are not the winning bid. They are the winning bid plus a fee the buyer pays the house, called the buyer's premium. The fee is large, it changes from time to time, and it is charged in bands, so it is worth knowing how it works before comparing any two prices.

What the premium is

The premium is a percentage of the hammer price that the buyer pays on top of it. It does not go to the seller. Each house publishes its own rates, and they are not negotiable in the ordinary way. The rates below leave out VAT and local sales taxes, which can be added to the premium.

The rates at the three largest houses

All three now charge the same way: a higher rate on the first band of the hammer price, a lower rate on the next band, and a lower rate again above that. These are the standard rates for London sales in pounds and New York sales in dollars.

Phillips also offers lower rates, of 25%, 20% and 14%, to a bidder who leaves a binding written bid at or above the low estimate at least 48 hours before a live sale. Its watch sales use different rates. Bonhams prints its terms with each sale, and they differ from sale to sale, so check the sale itself.

How the bands work

Each rate applies only to the part of the hammer price that falls inside its band, the way income tax bands work. Take a hammer price of £2,000,000 at Sotheby's in London. The first £1,500,000 is charged at 28%, which is £420,000. The remaining £500,000 is charged at 22%, which is £110,000. The premium is £530,000, and the published price is £2,530,000 before any tax.

Working back to the hammer price

When a house publishes only the total, you can often work the hammer price back, as long as you know the rate that applied on the day. Below the first threshold it is a single division.

  • Unspoken by Zara Muse sold at Christie's London online on 21 October 2026 for £30,720 with premium. Christie's 28% rate applied, so the hammer price was £30,720 divided by 1.28, which is £24,000.
  • Ava by Zara Muse sold at Christie's London online on 2 July 2026, before the rate changed, for £44,450. At 27% the hammer price was £35,000.
  • Kerze (Candle) by Gerhard Richter sold at Christie's New York on 20 May 2026 for US$35,135,000. Under the rates then in force, a hammer price of US$30,000,000 gives exactly that total: 27% of the first $1.5m, 22% of the next $6.5m and 15% of the remaining $22m. The Art Newspaper reported the hammer price as about US$30 million.

A round answer is a useful check. Bids rise in fixed steps, so a real hammer price is almost always a round number. If your answer is not, the rate, a tax or a discount is probably different from the one you assumed. On this site a hammer price worked out this way is marked "Our calculation".

We do not work back Phillips results for now, because the lower priority bidding rates mean two lots in the same sale can carry different premiums. One Phillips result does show how its figures are built, though. Banksy's Keep Ou T sold in London in October 2026 for £1,612,500. That is exactly a hammer price of £1,250,000 plus Phillips' standard 29%, which suggests that Phillips' "Sold For" figure includes the premium even though its lot page does not say so.

Why rate changes matter when you compare prices

The same hammer price gives a different total after a rate change. A £24,000 hammer at Christie's in London came to £30,480 with premium under the 27% rate, and £30,720 under the 28% rate. The difference is small here, but it grows with the price and with the band limits, which have also moved. When two totals from different years are close, part of the gap can be the fee, not the bidding.

The Artist's Resale Right

In the UK, a resale of a work through an auction house or dealer can also carry a royalty for the artist, or for their heirs for 70 years after death. Since 1 April 2024 it applies when the sale price is £1,000 or more, and it is worked out in bands:

  • 4% of the first £50,000
  • 3% from £50,000.01 to £200,000
  • 1% from £200,000.01 to £350,000
  • 0.5% from £350,000.01 to £500,000
  • 0.25% above £500,000, with the total capped at £12,500 for any one sale

On a sale price of £100,000 that comes to £2,000 plus £1,500, which is £3,500. The government guidance does not say who pays. The law firm Foot Anstey says the seller is legally responsible and that the royalty is worked out on the price before VAT and the buyer's premium. In practice each house's conditions of sale say how it is charged, so read those for the sale in question. The royalty is collected by the Design and Artists Copyright Society or the Artists' Collecting Society.

What the seller receives

None of these figures tells you what the seller was paid. The seller receives the hammer price less a commission agreed privately with the house, and less any agreed costs. Our article on liquidity works through an example.
