Guarantees, Irrevocable Bids and the Symbols in a Catalogue

By the Global Art Monitor research desk. 6 minute read.

Some lots arrive at auction already backed by a promise made before the sale. That promise changes what the final price tells you, so it is worth knowing how to spot it.

The house guarantee

With a house guarantee, the auction house promises the seller a minimum price whatever happens in the room. If the lot sells for less, the house makes up the difference. If it does not sell, the house pays the guaranteed sum and owns the work. The seller gets certainty, and the house wins a consignment it might otherwise have lost to a rival.

The third party guarantee, or irrevocable bid

A house can pass that risk to someone else. Before the sale, a third party commits to buy the lot at an agreed figure, usually through a written bid that cannot be withdrawn. Boodle Hatfield, a London law firm, describes this as an irrevocable written bid placed for a fee, for an amount that is not disclosed. If no one bids higher, the third party buys the work. If someone does, the third party is paid a fee, or a share of the price above the guarantee. Phillips' guide for buyers says the fee can be fixed or a percentage of the hammer price.

Christie's notes that a third party who wins the lot still pays the full hammer price and premium. Boodle Hatfield points out that the fee can work as a discount, so the price published for the lot may not be what the guarantor paid in the end. It also says guarantees tend to sit close to the low estimate.

How to spot them

Catalogues mark these lots with symbols, explained in a key printed with the conditions of sale. Phillips, for example, uses:

  • ○ for a lot Phillips has guaranteed itself.
  • ♦ for a lot with a third party guarantee.
  • Δ for a lot Phillips owns in whole or in part.
  • • for a lot offered with no reserve.

Other houses use their own symbols, so check the key in the catalogue you are reading. The auctioneer may also announce at the start of a sale that some lots are guaranteed. The amount of a guarantee is never published.

Why it matters when you read a result

  • The lot was always going to find an owner. If nobody bids, the house or the guarantor takes it, so a sale is not by itself proof of open demand.
  • One bid may be the whole story. If the guarantor is the only bidder, the result shows one party's pre-agreed price.
  • The guarantor knows more than you. Boodle Hatfield notes concerns that guarantors are often a house's biggest clients, and that they know the guaranteed figure when the room does not.

None of this makes a guaranteed result false. It is a real sale at a real price. It is one more thing to ask about before reading much into it.

What our tables show

Our tables do not yet record whether a lot was guaranteed, because most results pages do not repeat the catalogue symbols. Where we can see a symbol on the lot page, we will add it to the notes for that artist.
