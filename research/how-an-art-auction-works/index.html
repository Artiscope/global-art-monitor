How an Art Auction Works, From Consignment to Collection

By the Global Art Monitor research desk. Published 23 November 2026. 8 minute read.

Every price on this site is the last step of a process that starts months before the sale. Knowing the steps makes the figures easier to read, and it shows where the gaps in a published result come from.

1. Consignment

The owner, called the consignor, agrees to let an auction house offer the work. Between them they settle the estimate, the reserve and the seller's commission. The commission is agreed privately and is almost never published, which is one reason a hammer price does not tell you what the seller took home.

Some sellers are also given a guarantee: a promise that the work will sell for at least an agreed sum, whatever happens in the room. How that works, and how to spot it, is covered in our guide to guarantees and catalogue symbols.

2. The catalogue and the estimate

The house publishes an entry for each lot with a low and a high estimate. Phillips' guide for buyers in New York says a bid inside that range "should, in our opinion, offer a chance of success", and that estimates do not include the buyer's premium. An estimate is the house's opinion, and it is also a sales tool. It is not a valuation.

The entry also gives the house's view of who made the work, its condition and its history. Sotheby's states that these are statements of opinion. Our guide to provenance, authentication and condition explains what sits behind them.

3. The reserve

The reserve is the confidential lowest price the seller will accept. Phillips says its reserves are generally set at a percentage of the low estimate and never above it. The figure is not published, before the sale or after it.

Below the reserve, the auctioneer can bid for the seller. Phillips' guide says the auctioneer may open the bidding on the seller's behalf and keep bidding for the seller up to the reserve. A commentary from the Center for Art Law describes these as bids taken "off the chandelier": bids from nobody in the room, used to keep things moving and to hide where the reserve sits. Someone watching a sale usually cannot tell which early bids are real.

4. Bidding

There are four usual ways to bid: in the room with a numbered paddle, by telephone through a member of staff, online, or with an absentee bid left with the house beforehand. Phillips asks for absentee bids at least 24 hours before the sale, each with a maximum that leaves out the premium. Its staff then try to buy at the lowest price that the reserve and the other bidders allow. If two absentee bids match, the one received first wins.

Bids go up in steps called increments. Phillips uses steps of up to about 10 per cent, at the auctioneer's discretion.

5. The hammer

When bidding stops above the reserve, the auctioneer brings the hammer down. The winning bid is the hammer price. It is the figure the seller's proceeds are worked out from, and the figure the buyer's premium is charged on.

6. The premium and other charges

The buyer then pays the buyer's premium on top of the hammer price, plus any tax. At Christie's, Sotheby's and Phillips the standard rate on the first band of the hammer price is now 28 or 29 per cent. Our guide to the buyer's premium sets out the current rates and shows how to work a hammer price back from a published total.

In the UK, a resale of work by a living artist, or by one who died less than 70 years ago, can also carry the Artist's Resale Right. This is a royalty on a sliding scale that starts at 4 per cent, and lots it applies to are marked in the catalogue.

7. If the lot does not sell

If bidding stops short of the reserve, the lot is "bought in", also called passed. It goes back to the seller, or to the house if the house guaranteed it. Unsold lots often drop out of results lists and press reports. The Center for Art Law article cites a 2008 study which found that works that failed at auction later sold for about 30 per cent less. We have not read that study, so treat the figure as reported. What is clear is that an unsold lot tells you something about demand, which is why each artist profile on this site lists the unsold lots we can document.

8. Payment and collection

Phillips' New York guide asks for payment straight after the sale, unless other terms were agreed in writing beforehand. The buyer then collects or ships the work. Terms differ between houses and salerooms, so the conditions of sale printed with each catalogue are the rule book for that sale.

What reaches the published result

Of everything above, a published result usually shows two figures: the estimate and the price with premium. The reserve, the seller's commission, any guarantee and the number of bidders stay private. That is why we treat each result as evidence of one sale on one day. What an auction result actually tells collectors is a good next read.
