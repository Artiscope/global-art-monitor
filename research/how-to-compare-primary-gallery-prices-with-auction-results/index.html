How to Compare Primary Gallery Prices with Auction Results

By the Global Art Monitor research desk. Published 11 November 2026. 6 minute read.

Collectors are often shown an auction result as proof that a gallery price is fair, or a bargain. The two numbers can be compared, but only with care, because they come from markets that work in different ways.

Two markets, two kinds of price

The primary market is the first sale of a work, usually through the artist's gallery. The price is set in advance, tends to follow the size of the work, and moves slowly. Galleries generally raise prices in steps and avoid cutting them.

The secondary market is every sale after that, including auctions. Auction prices are set by whoever turns up to bid. They can jump or fall from one sale to the next.

A gallery price is an asking price that someone has agreed to. An auction price is the result of a contest. One is steady by design. The other is not.

A method in six steps

  1. Match the work. Compare paintings with paintings and prints with prints. Match size, year and series as closely as you can. A unique canvas and an edition of fifty are different goods.
  2. Use the right auction figure. If you are asking "what would I pay?", use the price with buyer's premium. If you are asking "what would I get if I sold?", start from the hammer price and take off a seller's commission.
  3. Put both in the same currency and on the same tax basis. Check whether the gallery price includes VAT and whether a converted auction price used the rate on the day of the sale.
  4. Look at all the results. Use every comparable sale, including the low ones and any unsold lots, not only the best.
  5. Check the dates. A result from two years ago and a price list from this month are not describing the same moment.
  6. Write down what you do not know. Who bid, who consigned, whether the lot was guaranteed, and what condition it was in.

Reading the gap

When auction results sit above gallery prices, it can mean demand is running ahead of supply. It does not mean you could buy at the gallery and sell at that auction price. You would receive the hammer price less commission, on a different day, with different bidders, for a different work.

When auction results sit below gallery prices, it does not prove the gallery is overcharging. Galleries sell with services attached: choice of work, provenance from the studio, and a relationship. It does tell you that resale may not return what you paid.

When there are only a few results, the honest reading is that the comparison is not yet reliable.

A caution about interested parties

Galleries, artists and existing collectors all benefit when an artist's auction prices are strong, and any of them may bid. That is a normal feature of the art market, and it is one reason a result cannot be read as a neutral measure of outside demand. It applies to the artists covered on this site as much as to any other. Our own position is set out on the about page.

The short version

Compare like with like, use the right figure for the question you are asking, and treat a small number of results as a small amount of evidence. For anything that turns on a specific figure, such as insurance or a sale, speak to a qualified valuer.
