New York, November 2026: The Top Lots and Why They Sold

By the Global Art Monitor research desk. Published 23 November 2026. 9 minute read.

Between 16 and 19 November 2026 the three largest auction houses held their autumn evening sales in New York. A Van Gogh made US$190.4 million, a new record for the artist, and records also fell for Kandinsky, Kusama and Sam Francis. Below are the main sales, the top lots and, as far as the sources allow, why they made what they did.

How to read the figures

Most prices in this piece were reported by the press, not read on the auction house's own lot page. Sotheby's shows results only to signed-in users, and Christie's lot pages refused our automated visits this week. Where only a reported, rounded figure exists we say so. Two exceptions in the top lots: Phillips' lot page gives the exact price for the Modigliani, and Christie's lot page for Kandinsky's Betonte Ecken, read after this report first appeared, gives US$85,780,000.

Every figure is in US dollars, because every sale here was in New York. "With fees" means the hammer price plus the buyer's premium, the fee the buyer pays the house. In New York this autumn Christie's and Sotheby's both charged 28% of the hammer up to US$2 million, 22% from there to US$8 million and 15% above. Our guide to the buyer's premium explains the bands.

Why the top lots made what they did

No report can say for certain why a bidder paid a price. What the sources do show is a set of features that the highest prices share, and that the lower ones often lacked.

The moment in the artist's life

Van Gogh painted Châtaigniers en fleurs in Auvers on 24 May 1890, according to Sotheby's lot page, about nine weeks before he died. He made about 70 oil paintings in roughly as many days in Auvers, so the supply of work from those weeks is small. The painting also has a long public history: the lot page lists exhibitions from Vollard's gallery in Paris in 1896 to the Fondation Louis Vuitton this year. It beat the previous Van Gogh record, US$117.2 million for Orchard with Cypresses at Christie's in 2022, by more than US$70 million.

Kandinsky's Betonte Ecken comes from his first years teaching at the Bauhaus, when his painting turned to hard-edged geometric forms. Kusama's No. 3P.B., which set her record at US$15 million with fees on 16 November, is one of her earliest Infinity Nets and was shown at the Whitney Museum in 1961.

Provenance and long ownership

Many of the top works had been off the market for decades, and their history of ownership was published in detail. Betonte Ecken was bought by Hilla Rebay in 1929 and given to the Guggenheim in 1941. The museum sold it in London in 1964 for £22,000, and it sold again at Sotheby's New York in 1986 for US$1 million, according to Artsy and Art Insider. At the Baum sale, Joan Mitchell's Green Book of Barbara Guest made US$19.3 million with fees. The Baums had bought it in 1986 for US$65,000.

At Phillips, Robert Manley told The Art Newspaper that nearly 90% of the works offered had never been sold at auction before. A work that is new to the market has no earlier price to anchor the bidding.

Clear title

The Van Gogh nearly did not sell cleanly. Heirs of the Berlin publisher Samuel Fischer, who owned it from 1909, said the family had been forced to sell it in 1936 under Nazi persecution. Sotheby's announced a settlement the day before the sale, and the lot was sold under an agreement between the seller and the Fischer heirs. A painting with an open claim against it is very hard to sell at any price. Our guide to provenance explains why.

Guarantees

A guarantee means that someone, the house or a third party, has promised to buy the work at an agreed price if no one bids more. At Christie's 20th Century sale 31 lots carried third-party guarantees and five were guaranteed by the house, out of 75. At Phillips 14 of 22 lots were guaranteed. Sotheby's lot page marks the Van Gogh as guaranteed property with irrevocable bids, and Artsy reports a third-party guarantee on the Kandinsky. A guaranteed lot is close to certain to sell, which helps explain high sell-through rates. It says less about how many people wanted the work. Our guide to guarantees and catalogue symbols has more.

Competition on the night

The Kandinsky opened at US$54 million and three bidders competed, according to Artsy. The Klimt went to a telephone bidder after nearly six minutes, said Christie's. The Modigliani rose from US$18 million to US$28 million within seconds and went on to a ten-minute contest between three bidders, The Art Newspaper reported. Two or more determined bidders are what take a price past its estimate.

Where prices fell short

The week was not uniformly strong, and the misses are part of the record.

  • Picasso's Le Baiser, consigned by Jeff Koons according to The Art Newspaper, made US$31.1 million with fees against an estimate of US$40 million to 60 million. Koons had bought it in 2008 for US$17.4 million.
  • Six of the twelve Blaquier-Arrieta lots sold below their low estimates, Art Insider reported. Pissarro's view of the Place du Théâtre-Français made a reported US$24.2 million against US$30 million to 40 million.
  • De Kooning's Water...Soft Banks and a Window (Untitled VIII) hammered at US$17.1 million, below its US$20 million low estimate.
  • Franz Kline's Figure (1955) hammered at US$1.8 million against US$4 million to 6 million at the Baum sale.
  • At Christie's 21st Century sale, bidding for Gerhard Richter's Abstraktes Bild (1990) stopped at US$5.7 million against US$6 million to 8 million, and it did not sell.

The New York adviser Ralph DeLuca put it simply to The Art Newspaper: estimates that week were "a little aspirational". A price below estimate does not mean a work is poor. It may mean the estimate was set high to win the consignment.

Records

Artsy counted 20 new artist auction records in the week. Beyond Van Gogh, Kandinsky and Kusama, they include Sam Francis (Towards Disappearance I, US$17.1 million), Aaron Douglas (Harriet Tubman, US$14.48 million against US$2.5 million to 3.5 million), Cornelis Cornelisz van Haarlem (Hercules and Achelous, US$13.9 million), Wifredo Lam (Ogue Orisa, US$11.6 million) and Fernando Botero (Horse, US$5.2 million). A record tells you the most one buyer has paid for one work. It is not a measure of an artist's market as a whole, as our piece on single record sales explains.

Elsewhere this autumn

In London on 14 October, Christie's two evening sales made £218.6 million, including fees, according to Apollo. The top lot was another Kandinsky, Das bunte Leben (1907), at £31,560,000 with fees. Working back from Christie's London rates gives a hammer price of exactly £27,000,000, which we show as our calculation on Kandinsky's profile. Mondrian's Composition III (1920) made £25,235,000 and Francis Bacon's Figure in Movement (1978) £17,185,000, as reported by London Art Walk. The next day Phillips sold all 20 lots in its London evening sale for £14,836,750, led by Richter's Mathis (1983) at £5,839,000, just under its £6 million low estimate, according to its own results. Sotheby's London contemporary evening sale on 15 October made £45.9 million with fees.

In Hong Kong, the autumn sales at Sotheby's, Christie's and Phillips at the end of September passed HK$2 billion together, the South China Morning Post reported, with HK$956.5 million at Christie's and HK$956 million at Sotheby's.

What these prices can and cannot tell you

They show what one buyer paid for one work on one evening, often with a guarantee behind it. They do not show what a different work by the same artist would make, and a week of records is not a trend. Several of the highest prices were for works with museum histories and decades in one family's hands, which few other works can match. Unique works and editions are kept apart throughout this site, and none of the lots above is an edition except the Giacometti bronze, which belongs to a small cast.
