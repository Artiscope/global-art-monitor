Provenance, Authentication and Condition: What Sits Behind a Price

By the Global Art Monitor research desk. 6 minute read.

An auction price is paid for more than an image. It is paid for a particular object with a particular history, and for the confidence a buyer has in who made it and what state it is in. Those three things, provenance, authentication and condition, explain many of the gaps between results.

Provenance

Provenance is a work's ownership history, from the studio to today. A clear record helps in two ways. It supports the attribution, and it shows that the work can be sold legally. A gap, especially in periods of war or upheaval, can make buyers wary. A distinguished owner, a museum loan or a well known exhibition in the record can add interest.

History can even change what a work is. In 2018 a Banksy painting, Girl with Balloon, shredded itself just after the hammer fell at a Sotheby's sale. Renamed Love is in the Bin, it was offered again at Sotheby's London on 14 October 2021. The Art Newspaper reported a hammer price of £16m and £18.6m with fees, against £1m at the 2018 sale.

What the catalogue wording means

Auction catalogues use set phrases to show how sure the house is. Sotheby's explains that the artist's name on its own means that, in its opinion, the work is by the artist. "Attributed to" means it is probably by the artist, with less certainty. Sotheby's also says that any statement about authorship, date, provenance or condition is a statement of opinion. The exact wording matters, and two lots with the same name at the top can be described with different confidence.

Authentication: the Banksy example

Some artists, estates or foundations run their own authentication. Banksy's is the clearest case among the artists on this site. His office, Pest Control, says it is the only body authorised to authenticate his work and issues certificates of authenticity. Its published terms say:

  • Work made in a public space, or removed from one, is not eligible. Street pieces are not authenticated.
  • Checking a screenprint costs £100 plus VAT, and an original £150 plus VAT. There is no charge if the work is found to be fake.
  • Pest Control decides at its own discretion and does not have to give reasons.
  • A buyer can ask it to check a work before buying, but only if the work already has a Pest Control certificate. New owners can register a change of ownership.

Condition

Damage, repair, fading and old varnish all affect what a buyer will pay. Houses will usually provide a condition report on request. Phillips says its staff who write them are not professional restorers or conservators, and encourages buyers to inspect the work or bring their own adviser. A condition report is a starting point, not a guarantee.

Reading results with this in mind

A database line rarely mentions provenance or condition. When two results for similar works are far apart, look at the lot pages. The difference is often there: a better history, a cleaner surface, or a firmer attribution.
