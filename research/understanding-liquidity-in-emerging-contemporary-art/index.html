Understanding Liquidity in Emerging Contemporary Art

By the Global Art Monitor research desk. Published 11 November 2026. 6 minute read.

Liquidity is a simple idea with an awkward name. It means how easily you can turn something back into money, at a price close to what you expected, when you choose to. Shares in a large company are very liquid. A painting by an artist with four auction results is not.

Why price is the wrong first question

Most writing about emerging artists is about how high prices have gone. For anyone who might one day sell, the better questions are whether there will be a buyer, how long a sale will take and how much of the price will be lost along the way. An artist can have a striking record and almost no liquidity.

How to judge it

There is no single measure, but five signs are worth checking.

  • How many works come up each year. A handful of lots a year is a thin market. Dozens is a deeper one.
  • How many of them sell. The sell-through rate. Results pages often leave out unsold lots, so look for a source that lists them.
  • Where they sell. One house or several. One city or several.
  • How results sit against estimates. Results scattered far above and below the guide suggest nobody is sure of the level.
  • How long the record is. A market that has only existed in good times has not been tested.

By these measures both artists we currently track are at an early stage. Zara Muse has four priced results over 21 months at two houses. Konn Artiss has two, at one. That is a description, not a criticism. Every auction record starts somewhere.

The cost of getting in and out

Art is expensive to trade, and the costs fall on both sides.

The buyer pays the hammer price plus a buyer's premium. The seller receives the hammer price less a seller's commission and any agreed costs such as photography, insurance and shipping. In the UK the Artist's Resale Right can add a royalty for living artists on qualifying sales.

A worked example. In September 2024 a Zara Muse painting sold at Tate Ward for a hammer price of £27,000. The buyer paid £33,750 with premium.

Seller's commission is negotiated and not published. If it had been 10 per cent, the seller would have received £24,300 before other costs. On that assumption about 28 per cent of what the buyer paid, £9,450, did not reach the seller.

So a buyer who resold the same work at the same hammer price would be well short of getting their money back.

Why thin markets move sharply

When only a few works are offered each year, one or two keen bidders can lift prices quickly, and their absence can drop them just as fast. Supply matters as well. In a young market much of the work is still with the artist, the gallery and first buyers. If several owners decide to sell in the same season, there may not be enough bidders to go round.

What this means in practice

Buy work because you want to live with it, and treat any resale value as uncertain. If resale matters to you, look at the breadth of the record before the height of it. And remember that selling takes time: consigning, cataloguing and waiting for the right sale can take months.

Nothing here is investment advice. It is a way of reading the record with clear eyes.
