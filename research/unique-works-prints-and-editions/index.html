Unique Works, Prints and Editions: Why We Keep Them Apart

By the Global Art Monitor research desk. 7 minute read.

Many of the artists on this site sell both unique works and editions. A search for an artist's name brings back both, side by side, and the gap between them can be very large. That is why our tables list unique works and name editions separately in the text.

What an edition is

Tate defines an edition as a set of identical copies made from a master, most often prints pulled from the same printing surface. Since the late 19th century most print runs have been limited and declared in advance. Each print is numbered: in an edition of 25, the prints are marked 1/25, 2/25 and so on. The bottom number is the size of the edition, and according to the International Fine Print Dealers Association it does not count the proofs.

Editions are not only prints. Sculpture, photographs and video are editioned too. The KAWS COMPANION sold at Christie's in June 2026 was number 3 in an edition of three, with two artist's proofs.

The letters you will see

  • AP, artist's proof. Prints made in addition to the edition, kept by the artist. Tate notes that they are the same as the edition prints.
  • PP, printer's proof. Copies given to the printer or publisher.
  • HC, hors commerce. French for "not for sale". The IFPDA says these are often used as exhibition copies and may or may not differ from the edition.
  • BAT, bon à tirer. "Good to print". The single proof the artist approves as the standard for the whole edition.
  • Trial proof. A working proof made before the plate or screen is finished. Tate is clear that this is not the same thing as an artist's proof.
  • Restrike. A later print not authorised by the artist or the artist's heirs.

Signed, or signed in the plate

The IFPDA says a print described simply as "signed" should be signed by hand in pencil, ink or crayon. A signature printed as part of the image is "signed in the plate", which is a different thing. It also draws a line between an original digital print, which the artist made as a print, and a digital copy of a work in another medium, which is a reproduction.

Editions are not interchangeable either

Copies of the same print do not share one price. The KAWS screenprint SHARE sold twice at Christie's online in 2026, once in pounds and once in dollars, and each sale is a separate result. Condition, framing, the sale and who happened to be bidding all play a part. A proof and a numbered print, or a signed and an unsigned copy, are different again.

Questions to ask of an edition result

  1. How large is the edition, and how many proofs were made besides?
  2. Is this copy numbered, a proof, or marked HC?
  3. Is it signed by hand, signed in the plate, or not signed?
  4. Is it an original print, or a reproduction of a work in another medium?
  5. How many other copies have sold, and for how much?
