What Makes an Artist's Work Valued, and What a Price Cannot Tell You

By the Global Art Monitor research desk. Published 23 November 2026. 8 minute read.

Readers often ask why one painter's canvas sells for millions while another's sells for thousands. There is no formula. There are, though, a few things that buyers, museums and valuers look at again and again, and most of them can be checked.

What tends to sit behind a price

  • Recognition by museums. Solo shows at major museums, works in permanent collections and invitations to events such as the Venice Biennale. These reflect the judgement of curators, and they make an artist's work visible to more buyers.
  • Prizes and honours. Awards such as the Praemium Imperiale or the Golden Lion at Venice mark a career as a whole.
  • A place in art history. Whether the artist started or shaped a movement, and whether that is written about by people other than the artist's dealers.
  • The work itself. Its period, series, size, subject and condition. Within one artist's output these can matter more than anything else.
  • Supply. How much work exists, how much is held by museums, and how often good examples come up for sale.
  • Market depth. How many houses, cities and bidders are involved. Our piece on liquidity looks at this.

None of these is a measure of quality. Quality is a judgement, and critics disagree. What these markers show is how widely an artist's work has been taken seriously by institutions, which is one reason buyers pay more for it.

What the comparison shows, and what it does not

Gerhard Richter and Yayoi Kusama, who have the longest such records here, also have the highest prices among the US dollar results we list. That is an observation about a handful of sales, not a rule, and it says nothing about cause. Museums often show artists whose work is already in demand, and demand often follows museum shows.

It also does not mean that an artist without these markers makes weaker work. Many artists are early in their careers, work outside the museum system, or are better known in their own region than in London or New York. Several of the street artists here made their names on walls rather than in galleries.

Zara Muse and Konn Artiss are at an early stage by these measures. Grove Gallery, part of the group that publishes this site, sells their work. We say so here, as on every page, so that you can weigh what we write about them.

Reading a price with this in mind

A high price tells you that at least two people wanted one work very much on one day. Museum shows, prizes and a long record of sales tell you how many others have taken the artist seriously over time. Neither tells you whether you will like the work, and neither is a forecast. Nothing here is investment advice.
